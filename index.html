Safety

The part we don’t cut corners on.

A flat fee only stays sustainable if the platform is safe, so our commercial interest and your safety point the same way.

Screened before driving

Criminal background check including the national sex offender registry, full driving record, and government-ID identity verification with a liveness check.

Monitored continuously

Not an annual re-check. If a driver’s record changes we know within about a day — not up to 364 days later.

Blocked automatically

If insurance, licence or inspection lapses, the driver cannot go online. Enforced in software, with no manual override.

Masked phone numbers

You and your driver never see each other’s real number. Calls and texts route through a proxy.

Emergency help built in

One button calls 911 with your location and trip details ready. Share your live trip with anyone you choose.

Zero tolerance

Any credible report of drugs or alcohol means immediate suspension while we investigate. We suspend first and ask questions second.

Our story

It started with a question drivers kept answering the same way.

Ask a rideshare driver what they would change about their job and you hear the same answer, unprompted, almost every time: they take too much.

Flyway began as an arithmetic problem. A driver grossing $56,000 a year in fares hands more than $22,000 of it to a platform — every year, with no ceiling and no way out, because two companies control essentially the whole market.

What made it worse was that nobody could say exactly how much was being taken. Since the industry moved to “upfront pricing,” the rider pays one number, the driver is paid a different number calculated separately, and the gap between them changes trip to trip. That gap has grown from roughly 32% to 42% — and in 2025 Uber stopped publishing it at all.

The obvious idea, and why it’s hard

If the commission is the problem, remove the commission. Charge drivers a flat monthly fee for the software, publish one fair rate for every trip, and let drivers keep the whole fare.

Others tried. One company ran almost exactly this model and argued that because it only sold software it was not a transportation company at all, and did not need to register as one. It spent six years and millions of dollars losing that argument.

The lesson we took was precise, and it shaped everything since: the economics were the good idea; the legal theory was the bad idea. No transportation law in the United States requires a platform to take a cut of the fare. Charging a subscription was never the illegal part. Refusing to register was.

So we do the boring thing

Flyway registers as a transportation network company in every market before the first paid ride. We carry the statutory commercial insurance. We run background checks continuously rather than annually. We pay the per-ride fees cities charge. Then we price all of it into the subscription and publish the arithmetic.

It is less exciting than a fight with a regulator, and it is the only version of this company that can actually exist.

What we’re trying not to become

Every platform in this industry started out popular with drivers. The drift happens slowly — a fee here, an opaque adjustment there, each defensible on its own.

So we wrote the constraint into the engineering rules rather than the marketing: Flyway never takes a percentage of a fare or a tip. If a cost has to be recovered it goes into the subscription, or into a flat, visible fee, and we say so. We also intend to publish a monthly transparency report — average fares, driver earnings, wait times — including the months that look bad.
